TerminalGB

A cycle-accurate Game Boy and Game Boy Color emulator, written in Rust, that runs on the desktop, in a terminal, and in a browser. Two picture engines, and why both ship

A Game Boy draws its picture one dot at a time. Almost every emulator does not: it waits until the end of a scanline, reads whatever the registers hold at that moment, and paints all 160 pixels of the line at once. That is fast, and for the overwhelming majority of software it is also correct, because most games do not change the picture registers halfway through a line.

TerminalGB carries both. standard is the whole-scanline engine, with mode 3 fixed at 172 dots. identical walks the real pipeline: the background fetcher, the eight-pixel FIFO, sprite fetches, the window restart, the SCX & 7 discard — and mode 3's length falls out of the machine rather than being asserted.

They are not a quality or an appearance setting. Palette, upscaler, transport and every other visual option live above the picture processor and are byte-identical either way; the accuracy harness renders 600 frames of dmg-acid2 and of Blargg's cpu_instrs through both and requires the framebuffers to match exactly. The denominator nobody publishes

29 of 79 on Mealybug Tearoom looks like a bad score, and quoting it without the next paragraph would be dishonest in the other direction. Mealybug is the suite that separates the two or three most accurate emulators in the world from everybody else; on the GB Emulator Shootout's own published per-test data for the 24 DMG rows, SameBoy — the reference implementation — scores 15.

More importantly, 45 of the 79 failing rows cannot be won by picture-processor work at all, and the breakdown is written down rather than waved at: 27 need the boot ROM's ® glyph sitting in video RAM, which is a licensing decision this project has deliberately not taken; 8 compare a greyscale render against a colour reference; and 7 are a ROM-versus-reference mismatch inside the suite itself. The honest denominator is 34.

Why the score is a pixel count, not a pass rate

The baseline records how many of the 23,040 pixels differ on every row, not just pass or fail. That matters because it lets progress show up when no row flips — a row going from 2,304 wrong pixels to 8 is real work, and a pass/fail column would report it as nothing at all. It also stops the reverse trick: a row that starts passing is a baseline change too, and CI is red on a change in either direction.

Two figures that used to sit on the project's own front page are out of date and are marked as such rather than quietly deleted: "Mooneye acceptance 13/66" and "SameSuite APU 2/61" both predate the timer rewrite, the five-machine-cycle interrupt dispatch and the timed OAM DMA.

A one-line filter that put 271 colours in a four-shade picture

This one is not an accuracy bug at all — it is a rendering bug, and it is the clearest example on this page of a fault whose cause is a single line of somebody else's code.

A terminal that speaks no image protocol gets the picture drawn out of the upper-half-block glyph ▀: the foreground colour paints the top half of a character cell, the background the bottom, two vertical pixels per cell. That is crisp by construction — a cell is two flat colours and there is nothing inside it that can be soft. And yet on GNOME-family terminals the picture was visibly blurry.

Both captures taken in Ptyxis 50.1 (VTE) at 80×24 cells, 2.8× scale, with Proto halfblocks — the same terminal on the same machine, one binary, the two render paths.

The softness was never in the glyph. It was in the sampling, and the cause was four lines down in a dependency:

Measured on dmg-acid2 at 80×24 cells: 271 distinct colours in a four-shade picture, 268 of which no Game Boy shade maps to. The fix samples the cells directly — nearest-neighbour at pixel centres, the same rule the video scaler already used — and writes a uniform cell as a space rather than ▀, so a font with a poor block glyph cannot soften the large flat areas a Game Boy picture is mostly made of.

The part that is genuinely counter-intuitive is the cost. Doing the sampling ourselves is cheaper: over roughly 1,070 frames at 146×54 cells, same ROM and window both sides, whole-frame time went 0.460 ms → 0.349 ms. The new path pays its full sampling cost on every single frame and still wins, because it replaces two image-crate resizes — a nearest upscale to the display's pixel size followed by a Triangle downscale back to the cell grid.

Blur has now been reported twice, so the regression guard asserts the property rather than the pixels: every colour on screen must be a colour that was in the framebuffer, which no interpolating resampler can satisfy. One unit test runs a one-pixel checkerboard — the worst case for a smoothing filter — at five window sizes; one end-to-end test drives the real render on dmg-acid2 at four.

The same trap, one repository over

PixelGB extracts map art from a running cartridge and guards its output on exactly this rule — no colour may appear that was not in the source — and cites this incident as the reason. A family that has been bitten once writes the assertion down.

Nine consoles, because a boot ROM is part of the silicon

The emulator picks its console from the cartridge header — a Color flag at $0143 boots a Game Boy Color, anything else boots a DMG, which is what a person with both machines on the shelf would do. What is less obvious is that auto, dmg, dmg0, mgb, cgb, cgb0, agb, sgb and sgb2 are nine different machines and not four with knobs.

A DMG 0, a Game Boy Pocket, a CGB 0 and a Game Boy Advance present identical hardware to a cartridge from PC=$0100 onwards. What differs is the state they hand it: A = $FF is how a game detects a Pocket, and bit 0 of B is how it detects an Advance and offers the brighter palette that screen needs. Mooneye ships a boot_regs, a boot_div and a boot_hwio ROM per machine, no two of which can pass at once, and all eleven are green.

Before this existed, everything booted a Color, so a DMG-only game always ran in the Color's compatibility mode — a genuinely different machine, with different boot registers (A=0x11 HL=0x007C rather than A=0x01 HL=0x014D) and the undocumented Color registers still answering. Nine Mooneye acceptance tests print those registers and can tell.

The Super Game Boy support is the place this project is most careful about how wide a claim sounds. All three of the shootout's published SGB rows pass, 0 of 23,040 pixels different, and of the eighteen emulators in the shootout's own table only GSE passes all three. That is real — and it is a much narrower claim than it reads as. All three of those ROMs are plain 160×144 grey screens, because each measures the same thing: the ICD2 command-packet receiver and the MLT_REQ joypad index, which is the only part of a Super Game Boy that a Game Boy program can read back. The border, the palettes and the SGB sound are one-way traffic to a screen the cartridge cannot see, so no test ROM can assert them and this emulator has nowhere to put them. The score is real and so is the limit; neither belongs in a sentence without the other.

The transfer controller drives the address bus too

Object DMA — the routine every Game Boy game uses to push sprite data into object memory — is usually modelled as a copy that takes 640 cycles and blocks the CPU from most of the memory map. That is the data half of one physical rule. The address half is the interesting one.

The transfer controller has no address bus of its own. While a transfer runs it drives the object-memory address lines, and the picture processor's object scan is on the other end of them. So for the eighty dots of the scan the PPU cannot read a single entry — its Y/X latch keeps whatever it last held, and all forty object slots are judged against that one stale pair.

Modelling that took the shootout score from 238 to 239 of 264 and Gambatte from 3,613 to 3,617 of 5,225 scored rows, with zero losses and everything else byte-identical on both engines. One row — but it was ashiepaws/strikethrough.gb, the first row that suite has ever scored here.

The part worth copying is how the one remaining free parameter was pinned. The controller takes the bus a fixed number of dots after the write to $FF46, and that number was derived from eight ROMs rather than chosen: Gambatte's late_sp{00,01,02,39}x_{1,2} each put the write one machine cycle either side of one scan slot, sp00x_2 finds object 0 while sp01x_1 misses object 1, and the sp02x/sp39x pair — same DMA phase, different slot — rules out every uniform shift. Then it was swept across the whole 811-row oamdma/ group:

The scan reads on even dots, so 1 and 2 are the same machine. That is what a derived constant looks like: a curve with a flat top two dots wide and a physical reason for the width, rather than the one value that made a favourite test go green.

The bug a hundred short test ROMs structurally cannot find

GBSelfTest is a sibling project: one self-contained cartridge that boots anywhere and runs a hundred-odd checks back to back on one machine. The very first thing its new object-DMA check did was find a real bug in TerminalGB, and the shape of that bug is the argument for the cartridge in a paragraph.

The transfer's idle arm — whose own comment claimed the default machine never reached it — cleared the memory unit's busy flag and not the picture processor's. It does reach it: the transfer's last machine cycle leaves the position counter at its limit inside the bus tick without the flag moving, so the instruction's remainder is where the transfer gets noticed to be over. The PPU's "the transfer owns object memory" flag then latched on for the rest of the session, and the object scan never read object memory again — 192 of 550 frames of a real cartridge.

Every c-sp, Mooneye, Mealybug and SameSuite row is byte-identical either way, because they run one short ROM each. A cartridge that runs a hundred checks back to back in one session is a shape no test ROM has.

TerminalGB's own working notes on the find

Thousands of rows across five public suites could not see it, and that is not a criticism of those suites — it is a property of their shape. Each ROM boots, measures one thing and stops. A latch that needs a transfer to end on an instruction boundary and a scan to follow it never gets the chance.

The reds that are meant to stay red

Two rows on the scoreboard are expected failures, and both are kept rather than excluded, because a suppressed red is indistinguishable from a fixed one six months later.

  • Blargg's oam_bug on a Color console. All eight sub-tests pass on DMG, where the object-memory corruption defect is modelled faithfully. Color silicon has no such defect, so the Color row is red and should be.
  • csp/bully. Its black screen was one failing check — the Color-mode boot system counter — and chasing it produced a genuinely instructive non-fix. The obvious move was to sweep the counter until bully went green. It was not taken, and not out of timidity: bully waits for LY >= $90 before reading the divider, so what it pins is the phase between the system counter and the picture processor at hand-off, not the counter alone — and no published ROM measures where a Color boot ROM leaves the PPU for a Color cartridge. Fitting one unknown to absorb another would have moved every Color cartridge's divider, and so its audio phase and its random number generator, on the evidence of a single row.

The counter did change later, when a second independent source arrived: Gambatte's div/start_inc_{1,2}_cgb04c hardware tests take the genuine Color path and pin both the divider's value and the exact machine cycle its low byte wraps on, at a fixed distance with no LY wait — so they measure the counter alone, which is precisely the confound the original objection named. Bully's row is still red, byte-identical in both picture modes, because the PPU half is untouched. Two crews' work, not one.

Where these numbers come from

Every suite score on this page is read from TerminalGB's own checked-in conformance baselines, which are re-run in CI on every push and are red on a change in either direction. The reference suites cited — Mooneye GB, SameSuite, Mealybug Tearoom, CasualPokePlayer, Gambatte, AGE and the GB Emulator Shootout — are public; the ROMs are never vendored, they are fetched at run time from pinned commits, and every verdict is the ROM's own or its author's reference image, never a hash of the emulator's previous output.

The half-block timing figures (0.460 → 0.349 ms) come from the emulator's own opt-in telemetry over roughly 1,070 frames at 146×54 cells, same ROM and window both sides. The two half-block screenshots were taken in Ptyxis 50.1 on the machine the fix was made on; the per-terminal matrix in the source marks which rows were actually executed there and which state only which code path a terminal takes.

TerminalGB ships no ROMs, no boot ROM and no extracted game assets. The screenshots here are of test ROMs the project is free to show.
